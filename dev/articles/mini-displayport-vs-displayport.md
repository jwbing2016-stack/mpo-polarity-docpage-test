# Mini DisplayPort vs. DisplayPort: Which Cable Do You Need?

If your laptop has Mini DisplayPort and your monitor has full-size DisplayPort, a direct Mini DisplayPort to DisplayPort cable is usually the simplest choice. Both connectors carry DisplayPort video. The plug shape tells you which cable will fit, while the source, display and cable together determine which resolution and refresh rate you can use.

## Identify the video output and the monitor input

Full-size DisplayPort is the wider connector with one beveled corner. It is common on desktop graphics cards, monitors and docks. Mini DisplayPort, often shortened to Mini DP or mDP, is the smaller connector found on some older laptops, compact workstations and graphics cards. If you're plugging into a dock, identify the output on the dock itself.

For a single monitor, start with the connector at each end:

- **Mini DisplayPort output to full-size DisplayPort input:** Use a [Mini DisplayPort to DisplayPort cable](https://cablefam.com/product/mini-displayport-to-dp-cable-1-4-8k-60hz). A direct cable keeps the connection in DisplayPort and needs no HDMI converter.
- **Mini DisplayPort on both devices:** Use a Mini DisplayPort to Mini DisplayPort cable.
- **Full-size DisplayPort on both devices:** Choose a [full-size DisplayPort cable](https://cablefam.com/product-c/audio-video/displayport-cables) rated for the link speed your setup needs.
- **Mini DisplayPort output to HDMI input:** Choose a Mini DisplayPort to HDMI adapter or adapter cable rated for your display mode. A passive adapter needs a source that supports Dual-Mode DisplayPort (DP++). Choose a compatible active converter if the source lacks DP++ or the passive adapter falls short of your target mode. Check the stated signal direction before ordering.

## Check the maximum mode for the exact ports

A Mini DisplayPort plug can fit an older workstation whose video output tops out below the monitor’s advertised maximum. For example, if you want 4K at 120 Hz, check the workstation or dock’s output specification and the monitor’s specification for its **DisplayPort input**. A monitor may advertise different limits for different inputs. If you need HDR or a particular color depth, check those requirements at the target refresh rate as well.

Then select a cable that supports the required link rate at the length you plan to buy. A higher-rated cable can provide the link quality a compatible setup needs. The device specifications still set the upper limit. For a rollout, test one complete setup at the target mode before ordering the remaining cables.

## Watch for Thunderbolt ports that look like Mini DisplayPort

Thunderbolt 1 and 2 share the Mini DisplayPort connector shape. On older Macs, a lightning-bolt symbol identifies the Thunderbolt port. These ports can output Mini DisplayPort video to a compatible DisplayPort monitor. A display that requires a Thunderbolt connection, such as the Apple Thunderbolt Display, needs a compatible Thunderbolt connection from the host. The same plug shape can therefore lead to two different cable choices.

If the host has USB-C, check whether that particular port supports DisplayPort video output. For a monitor with a full-size DisplayPort input, a compatible USB-C to DisplayPort cable is often the direct option.

## What to put on a purchase request

List the source and monitor models, the connector at each end, the required resolution and refresh rate, cable length and quantity. For example: **Mini DisplayPort to full-size DisplayPort, 2560 × 1440 at 60 Hz, 2 m, 24 workstations.** If a dock sits between the computer and monitor, include its model and the specific output you plan to use.
